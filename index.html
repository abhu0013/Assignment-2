The Evolution of the Game: Power Creep

While the physical locations of tournaments expanded globally, the cards themselves underwent a drastic historical shift. Over 25 years, the maximum Hit Points (HP) of Pokémon cards skyrocketed—a phenomenon known as "power creep." This forced players to constantly upgrade their collections to remain competitive.

The Elemental Balance

Beyond power inflation, the game's core mechanics rely on elemental types. By analyzing the proportion of types printed each year, we can see how the designers balanced the game—and identify eras where certain elements heavily dominated the competitive meta.

The Expanding Power Gap

By comparing historical expansion series, we can track the structural boundaries of the game. While the minimum Hit Points (HP) of basic Pokémon have remained relatively stable since the Wizards of the Coast era, the maximum HP ceiling has violently stretched. This growing chasm forces modern players to adopt highly polarized deck strategies.

The Cost of Competitiveness: HP by Rarity

Power creep is not distributed equally. By analyzing the statistical spread of Hit Points across distinct rarity tiers, it becomes clear that modern competitive viability is paywalled. The median HP of 'Ultra Rare' and 'Secret Rare' cards vastly outscales standard Common and Uncommon cards, leaving lower-tier cards statistically unviable in tournament play.

The Deck Ecosystem: Pokémon vs. Trainers

A competitive deck cannot function on Pokémon alone. By tracking the historical print volume across all three major card supertypes, we can see how the game's designers began printing a massive influx of 'Trainer' cards (items and supporters) in the modern era to balance out the overwhelming power creep of the Pokémon themselves.
